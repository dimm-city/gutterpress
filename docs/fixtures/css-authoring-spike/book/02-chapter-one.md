# Reading the Tides

![A heron waiting at low tide](assets/heron.jpg){.float-left}

The marsh reveals itself twice a day. At low tide, mudflats stretch out
toward the channel, pocked with the breathing holes of clams and the
zig-zag tracks of shorebirds. At high tide, the same ground disappears
under a foot of brackish water, and the foraging grounds become a
different place entirely — one you travel by boat, not boot.

Learning to read a tide table is the first skill any forager needs.
Coastal almanacs list two high tides and two low tides a day, roughly
twelve hours and twenty-five minutes apart, drifting later each day as
the moon's position shifts. A spring tide — the biggest swing between
high and low — happens near the new and full moon. A neap tide, the
smallest swing, happens at the quarter moons.

Timing a trip around a spring low tide exposes the most ground and the
most food: mussel beds normally submerged, cockle flats normally too
deep to reach on foot, eelgrass meadows where bay scallops shelter.
Arrive an hour before the predicted low and you get a widening window
as the water keeps retreating.

## Tools Worth Carrying

A short checklist, kept in the same dry bag every trip, saves more time
than any single piece of gear:

- A tide table for the current month, printed, not just on a phone
- A rake or clam gun for digging
- A mesh bag, so undersized catch drains and survives release
- Polarized sunglasses, to cut glare off wet sand
- A dated permit, where the local regulations require one

<aside class="callout">

**Know your limits.** Most coastal jurisdictions cap the daily take per
species and require a license for anyone over about sixteen. Rules
change by county and by season — check the current regulations before
every trip, not just the first one.

</aside>

@end-section

## Wind, Not Just Water

Wind direction matters as much as the tide chart. A strong onshore wind
can push the actual water level a foot or more above prediction, while
a hard offshore wind can strand a boat that timed its return around the
printed numbers alone. Watch the sky as closely as the table.
