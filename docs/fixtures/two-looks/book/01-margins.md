# Two Looks

Two looks set a page margin, 0.5in then 0.75in: every page prints with the later look's.

## The West Bank Ledger

The west bank ledger opens with the spring survey, taken on foot at the lowest tide of the season. Every stake line is walked twice, once at first light and once near noon, because the morning sun flattens the mudflat's relief and hides the drainage cuts that matter most to anyone who has to cross it later in the year.

A stake that has moved is not always a stake that has failed. Silt travels, and a channel that ran hard against the eastern bank one winter will have shouldered its way twenty feet west by the following autumn. The ledger records the position, the date, and the tide; it does not attempt to record the reason.

Rope is measured wet. A dry line reads short and flatters the surveyor, and a flattered surveyor sets his stakes too close together and runs out of bank before he runs out of rope. The rule is old enough that nobody remembers who wrote it down first, and sound enough that nobody has argued with it since.

The lantern is lit at the turn, not before. Burning it through the slack hours wastes oil and ruins the eye for the dark, and the dark is where the work happens once the tide begins to make. Two hours of good night vision are worth more than four hours of bad light, and cost considerably less.

## Stake Lines

Oyster ground is surveyed differently, by sound rather than by sight. A pole dropped on live bed rings; a pole dropped on dead bed thuds and keeps thudding. The distinction is obvious to anyone who has heard both, and impossible to convey in writing to anyone who has not, which is why the apprenticeship runs three years.

Charts age badly here. A chart is a claim about where the water was on a particular afternoon, and the water has no obligation to honour it. The prudent pilot carries the chart, reads the chart, and then watches the surface for the long smooth tongue of current that says the channel has moved again.
