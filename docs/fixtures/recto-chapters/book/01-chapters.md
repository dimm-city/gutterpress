# The Spring Survey

The west bank ledger opens with the spring survey, taken on foot at the lowest tide of the season. Every stake line is walked twice, once at first light and once near noon, because the flat midday sun hides the drainage cuts that matter most to anyone who has to cross the mud later in the year.

Rope is measured wet. A dry line reads short and flatters the surveyor, and a flattered surveyor sets his stakes too close together and runs out of bank before he runs out of rope. The rule is old enough that nobody remembers writing it down, and sound enough that nobody has argued with it since.

A stake that has moved is not always a stake that has failed. Silt travels, and a channel that ran hard against the eastern bank one winter will have shouldered its way twenty feet west by the following autumn. The ledger records the position, the date, and the tide; it does not attempt to record the reason.

Oyster ground is surveyed by sound rather than by sight. A pole dropped on live bed rings; a pole dropped on dead bed thuds and keeps thudding. The distinction is obvious to anyone who has heard both and impossible to convey in writing to anyone who has not, which is why the apprenticeship runs three years.
