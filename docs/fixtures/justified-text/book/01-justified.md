# Justified Text

A book set *justified*, with the ordinary inline markup of any manual:
emphasis, **strong words**, `inline code` and [links](#the-press-room).
Each of those splits a line into separate pieces of text, and every piece
is stretched to the column on its own, which is what this fixture exists
to exercise.

## The Press Room

The press room opens at *seven*, and the first job of the morning is the
one nobody wants: cleaning the **rollers** that were left inked overnight.
A roller left dry for a night takes an hour to bring back, and a roller
cleaned at once takes ten minutes, so the rule on the wall reads simply
`clean before you leave`, in letters large enough to read from the door.

Paper is stacked by *weight* and then by **grain**, never the other way
round, because a sheet folded across the grain cracks along the fold and
the whole signature has to be printed again. The stock list on the
[shelf card](#the-press-room) gives both, and the apprentice who files a
ream by colour learns the rule the first time a run of `80gsm` cover stock
splits along every spine in the batch.

Type is set in a *composing stick* and locked into a **chase** with wooden
furniture and quoins. A line that is too loose rattles and drops letters
during the run; a line that is too tight bows the forme and prints a
smudge across the middle of the page. The right pressure is learned by
ear, which is why the foreman taps each `quoin` before the forme goes on
the bed, and listens.

## Make-Ready

*Make-ready* is the slow part of every job and the part customers never
see. The pressman builds up the **packing** under the tympan sheet by sheet
until every letter on the forme prints with the same weight, adding
tissue under a light corner and cutting it away under a heavy one, then
pulling another proof, then adding `one more sheet`, often for an hour.

A good make-ready is *invisible*: the page looks evenly printed and nobody
asks why. A bad one shows as a **grey corner** or a dark word, and the
customer, who cannot name the fault, still knows the page is wrong. The
shop's [proof log](#make-ready) records every sheet added, so the next run
of the same forme starts from `last time` instead of from nothing.

Ink is mixed on the *slab* with a palette knife, a little at a time, and
tried on a scrap of the job's own **stock** before any goes on the press.
The same ink looks different on a coated sheet and an uncoated one, and a
colour matched on the wrong paper has to be matched again, which is why
the scraps live in a drawer labelled `test sheets` beside the slab.

## Folding and Binding

Printed sheets are folded into *signatures* of sixteen pages, gathered in
order, and sewn through the **fold**. The gatherer checks each signature
by the black mark printed on its spine, a short bar that steps down one
place per signature, so that a missing or doubled section shows as a gap
or a `double step` in the staircase of marks along the back of the book.

The sewn book block is *rounded* and backed, then glued and lined with
mull before the **case** is fitted. Each of those steps waits for glue to
set, so a run of a few hundred books takes the better part of a week even
in a shop that does nothing else, and the binder's
[schedule](#folding-and-binding) is written in days rather than hours, with
`drying` marked in red.

Trimming is the *last* cut and the one that cannot be undone. The guillotine
takes three sides of the book block at once, **head**, foot and fore-edge,
and a block set a millimetre askew loses a millimetre of margin on every
page. The binder measures the `trim size` twice against the printed crop
marks, sets the back gauge, and only then lowers the blade.

## Proofing

The proofreader reads *against copy*, aloud, with a second reader holding
the **manuscript** and following along. Every mark goes in the margin in
the shop's own shorthand, and every query the proofreader cannot settle is
flagged with a `query` mark for the author rather than corrected on a
guess, because a guess that is wrong is worse than a question left open.

A second proof follows the *corrections*, and the reader checks only the
marked lines and the **lines around them**, since a correction that adds a
word can push the rest of the paragraph down and break a line somewhere
else. The [proof log](#proofing) holds both proofs side by side, and the
job is not passed for press until the second matches the marks of the
first, line for line, with `passed` written across the top.
